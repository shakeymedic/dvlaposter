Fitness to drive

Which conditions need action, and what to tell the patient

Full DVLA guidance for clinicians (GOV.UK)
It is the patient's legal duty to tell DVLA. Our job is to advise, signpost and document. DVLA decides whether, and when, the patient can drive. Rules change, so check the current guidance (QR code) for timings.

1 Advise

If the patient has a condition on this poster, explain that it may affect their driving and that they have a legal duty to tell DVLA.

Advise them not to drive where this poster says stop.

2 Signpost

Ask them to look up their condition on GOV.UK, or to tell DVLA and let DVLA tell them when they can drive.

For patients: check if a health condition affects your driving

3 Document

Record the advice in the notes, for example:

"DVLA driving advice given. Advised not to drive and to inform DVLA. Patient understands."

4 Escalate if needed

If the patient cannot or will not stop driving and others are at risk of death or serious harm, speak to a senior about telling DVLA without consent (rare; GMC guidance).

If they cannot understand the advice (for example dementia), DVLA should be told.

Group 1

Cars and motorcycles. Most patients. DVLA threshold: 20% risk of a sudden disabling event in a year.

Group 2

Large lorries (C) and buses (D). Much stricter: 2% risk a year. Some older car licences include medium lorries and minibuses (C1, D1).

Always ask: "Do you drive a lorry, bus or minibus, or drive for work?"

Taxi and emergency service drivers may have higher standards set by their licensing authority or employer.

  2. Stroke, Group 1: tell DVLA if any deficit remains after 1 month (for example visual field, cognition or limb function).
  3. Head injury, Group 1: no need to tell DVLA after full recovery if amnesia lasted 24 hours or less, there was no seizure (other than at impact) and CT showed no haematoma or contusion. Otherwise, longer off driving: check the guidance.
